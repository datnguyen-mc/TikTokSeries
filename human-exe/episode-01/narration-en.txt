Ben automated his entire job. By Monday, he was working twelve minutes a day. By Friday, his boss wanted to know his secret. That was the problem.

Three weeks earlier, Ben's job had been moving information between rectangles. An email became a spreadsheet. The spreadsheet became a report. The report became a presentation explaining the spreadsheet. Every Thursday, someone asked for the same information in a slightly different rectangle. Ben had a university degree and a favorite copy-and-paste shortcut. Only one of them came up every day.

So Ben tried something. He built a little AI workflow using company-approved tools and dummy data. It sorted a sample inbox, drafted a summary, and filled a practice report. Then Ben checked it. The first version confidently put a sandwich under business expenses. The second invented a customer called Customer. Ben fixed the workflow, added checks, and kept a human review at the end. He still handled the unusual requests. He still had to understand the numbers. The machine could move a mistake very quickly, which was different from making the mistake disappear. Eventually, the boring part got shorter. Much shorter.

On Monday, the usual reports were finished before his coffee cooled. Ben checked the numbers. Correct. Checked the attachments. Correct. Checked whether he was allowed to feel happy. Unclear. He took a real lunch break. He called his mother. He went outside and discovered that daylight was available on weekdays. For the first time in months, he finished work with enough energy to have a life after it.

Then his boss, Mara, noticed. She asked how he had become so productive. Ben considered saying vitamins. Instead, he showed her the workflow. Mara watched a day's paperwork turn into a short review. She smiled. Ben smiled. Somewhere, a very small alarm went off in Ben's brain. The next morning, he received the work of three people. Same desk. Same salary. Bigger rectangle.

Ben made the workflow faster. The workload grew again. He removed another repetitive step. Another department appeared. Soon, Ben wasn't doing less work. He was supervising a tiny digital factory while answering messages about why the factory wasn't faster. Last month's miracle became this month's minimum. His dashboard turned green. His lunch went cold. Then an unusual request arrived. It needed a decision the workflow couldn't make. Ben had to stop the belt and think. A message appeared asking why the belt had stopped. Apparently, thinking was now a delay. The button had saved him hours. Somehow, those hours already belonged to someone else.

On Friday evening, Mara called him into a meeting. Ben expected a warning. Instead, she closed the door and turned her laptop around. She had automated her reports too. Her boss had responded by giving her two more teams. Mara wasn't standing outside the machine. She was on the next conveyor belt. They stared at each other. Two people who had saved time, both too busy to enjoy it.

They could hide the tools. They could pretend everything still took eight hours. Or they could ask a more uncomfortable question: when a task gets faster, what are we actually trying to improve? They wrote down three possibilities. More output. Better quality. More time back. All three sounded good. But the calendar had room for only one by default. So they decided to make the choice visible.

On Monday, they proposed a small trial. Keep the review checks. Agree on a realistic amount of work. Use some saved time to fix recurring mistakes. Protect a little of it from being instantly filled. The first response was another request for more output. Mara put the error log beside the green dashboard. Ben showed the requests that still needed human judgment. They weren't asking everyone to stop working. They were asking the plan to include the work the dashboard couldn't see. It wasn't a dramatic speech. It was a very ordinary meeting with a very unusual slide: time saved should lead to a decision, not an automatic increase in workload. The team agreed to try it for a month, then review both the results and the workload. Nobody got a private island. Ben got a warm lunch.

The tool hadn't decided how Ben's time would be used. People had. And that meant people could decide differently. A faster workflow can help. It can also make an impossible workload look temporarily possible. The upgrade is not just the button. It's the agreement around the button. On Friday, Ben closed his laptop at five. Then his phone lit up. Could you automate our meetings? He looked at Mara. Mara looked at the calendar. For once, they scheduled a conversation before building another machine. If you saved ten hours at work, who would get them?
