## Description
In the autumn of 1918, an invisible virus infected around 500 million people – one in every three people on Earth. It took more lives than the whole of the First World War, and strangest of all, it hit the young and healthy hardest.

Why was it wrongly called the "Spanish" flu? Why did a parade in Philadelphia turn into a disaster, while St. Louis acted early and saved lives? Why did American Samoa lose no one, while Western Samoa lost nearly a fifth of its people? And how did scientists bring back a virus that had vanished – from the Alaskan permafrost?

👉 If you had been Philadelphia's health director in 1918, would you have cancelled the parade? Tell me in the comments!

⚠️ This video uses simple animated illustrations to retell historical events; real people appear only as simple labelled drawings. No bodies are shown. Some figures (total deaths, the virus's origin) remain estimates and are still debated by scientists.

## Notes on content
- Infected: about 500 million (≈ 1/3 of the world). Deaths: estimates 17–100 million, most often quoted ~50 million (CDC).
- Philadelphia vs St. Louis: peak weekly excess pneumonia & influenza death rate 257 vs 31 per 100,000 (Hatchett et al., 2007).
- Western Samoa: ~8,500 deaths (~22% of the population); American Samoa: no recorded flu deaths while the quarantine held.
- The 1933 sneezing-ferret story is an often-told anecdote.
