## Description
January 15, 1919. On a strangely warm winter day in Boston, a giant steel tank on the waterfront bursts open. More than two million gallons of molasses rush through the North End in a wave taller than a house.

It sounds like a joke – but 21 people lost their lives and about 150 were injured. And the company behind the tank tried to blame someone else.

In this episode: a tank that was never properly tested, leaks painted brown, rivets flying like bullets, a six-year court case – and the science that finally explained why molasses can be so deadly.

## Notes on content
- Figures follow the main accounts: 2.3 million gallons, a tank about 50 ft tall and 90 ft wide (built 1915), a wave up to 25 ft at about 35 mph, 21 dead and about 150 injured.
- The company blamed anarchists; after years of hearings the court-appointed auditor ruled in 1925 that the tank was unsafe, and the company paid about $628,000.
- The 2016 Harvard study used corn syrup in a cold room to show how cooling molasses thickened and trapped people.
- Only the company treasurer is named, as a historical figure; victims are not named.
